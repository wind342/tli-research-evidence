## Abstract

Despite the remarkable success of neural networks, their internal mechanisms remain largely a black box. What determines the outcomes of training? Why can subsequent training of the same model, with identical parameters and training data, still yield different results? What internal changes occur in a model during learning? What is a neural network's capability, and why can capabilities be acquired, lost, or recovered during training? How are inference and learning related? To address these questions, we conducted systematic causal experiments across six neural-network systems, progressively establishing the fundamental relations among training, learning, and inference (TLI) and validating them across architectures. We found that the same realized training update can produce different functional responses in different receiving states and exhibit state-dependent nonlinear behavior; during learning, distributed functional support within neural networks undergoes reorganization; capability is a target-specific decision on a continuous functional readout, with its acquisition, loss, and recovery determined by functional responses crossing decision boundaries; and inference recruits and combines functional support formed through learning while preserving the learned state. Based on the established TLI relations, we developed a prediction algorithm that uses pre-update information and an already formed training update to predict post-update target correctness and capability transitions without accessing actual post-update target outputs. The algorithm achieved prediction accuracies ranging from 91.43% to 99.87% across six neural-network systems. Building on these findings, we further found in a seventh, reinforcement-learning system that even when feedback is consistently correct, concentrating it on one capability shifts internal functional support toward the reinforced capability and weakens other previously acquired capabilities; rebalancing feedback redistributes that support and restores those capabilities. On the basis of these experiments, we established an abstract, unified system of coupled dynamical equations describing these relations. This experimentally established system of abstract dynamical equations exhibits broad mathematical unity. By substituting only the mathematical models and conditions already used in the respective original studies into the same equations, straightforward calculations and derivations recover mathematical relationships across fourteen established theories, including optimization stability, implicit bias, spectral learning, generalization, catastrophic forgetting, grokking, scaling laws, and double descent. We also found that the core relationships in these equations can be explained by the most elementary computations of neural networks. These findings advance our understanding of neural networks from black-box behavior toward unified internal mechanisms.
